Introduction & motivation

The challenge

  • On one side, data from the real world: case counts, hospital admissions, deaths
  • On the other, mathematical models such as SIR and SEIR
  • How do we connect the two?

The data we will use today

  • Influenza in a boarding school in England, January 1978
  • 763 pupils, one of whom brought the virus back from holiday

The data we will use today

  • The school recorded how many pupils were in bed each day for 14 days
  • Nobody left or arrived, and every case was seen, which makes this about as clean as outbreak data get

The model we will use today

\[\begin{align} \frac{dS}{dt} &= -\beta SI \\ \frac{dI}{dt} &= \beta SI - \gamma I \\ \frac{dR}{dt} &= \gamma I \end{align}\]

  • \(S\), \(I\), \(R\) count pupils, so \(S + I + R = N = 763\)
  • Give the model 762 susceptible pupils and 1 infectious pupil on day 0 and it draws a curve of \(I(t)\) for us to compare with the data
  • The quantities we actually want come from these two:
    • \(R_0 = \beta N / \gamma\), the basic reproduction number
    • \(1 / \gamma\), the mean infectious period in days
  • Two unknowns: \(\beta\), the transmission rate, and \(\gamma\), the recovery rate

The calibration problem

Parameter estimation and uncertainty quantification

  • Some guesses are obviously wrong and some are close, but by eye we cannot rank the close ones. We need a rule that says which is best
  • A best guess on its own is worth little without knowing how sure we can be. Would \(\gamma = 0.4\) have done just as well as \(0.5\)?
  • Once we have both, we can estimate \(R_0\), forecast the second week, or ask what closing the school early would have changed

Discussion

  • Look at the three guesses again. Which of the two parameters do you think the data pin down better, and why?
  • What else could the school have recorded in 1978 that would help us estimate \(\gamma\) directly?

Approaches to parameter estimation

There are many approaches to answer the question but we’ll cover:

  1. Least squares: measure how far the curve is from the data and make that distance as small as possible. Simple and fast, but it says nothing reliable about uncertainty
  2. Maximum likelihood: measure the probability of the data under the model instead. This gives us confidence intervals and a way to compare models
  3. Bayesian inference: ask how plausible every value is, rather than which single value is best. This lets us use what we already know, and gives uncertainty that still makes sense for awkward models. The cost is that the answer has to be sampled, with MCMC

We finish with a short look at what to do when even the likelihood cannot be written down

Conceptual foundations

Definition of model fitting

Choose the parameters \(\theta = (\beta, \gamma)\) so that the model’s output is as close as possible to the data \(\mathbf{y}\)

  • Everything depends on what we mean by close
  • Whatever we decide, we write it as a number: an objective function \(\mathcal{L}(\theta, \mathbf{y})\) that is small when the curve is near the data and large when it is far away
  • Fitting then means searching over \(\theta\) for the values that make that number small

Two formulations of the fitting problem

Point estimation

\[\hat{\theta} = \arg\min_{\theta} \mathcal{L}(\theta, \mathbf{y})\]

The next two sections use two different choices of \(\mathcal{L}\), the sum of squared errors and then the negative log-likelihood

Posterior distribution

A probability for every \(\theta\) given the data, rather than the one best value

\[p(\theta \mid \mathbf{y})\]

This is Bayesian inference, and it is where the lecture ends up. The two answers share the likelihood, which we build in section 4

Sources of uncertainty

Even with the right \(\beta\) and \(\gamma\) the curve will not pass through every point. There are several reasons for the mismatch, and the lecture deals with each of them somewhere

Identifiability

The problem: different parameter combinations can produce nearly the same curve

  • A high \(\beta\) with a high \(\gamma\): fast spread, quick recovery
  • A low \(\beta\) with a low \(\gamma\): slow spread, long infections
  • Both can give a similar epidemic, because what the data pin down is mostly the ratio \(R_0 = \beta N / \gamma\)

We will meet this three times today: as a tilted valley in the least squares surface, as wide profile likelihoods, and as a tilted cloud of posterior samples

Remedy: bring in what you already know, for instance a plausible infectious period for influenza. In the Bayesian section that is exactly what a prior is

Least squares estimation

The least squares criterion

The residual for each day is the difference between the observation and the model prediction. The least squares criterion is the sum of squared residuals, the SSE

\[\text{SSE} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \bigl(y_i - f(t_i, \theta)\bigr)^2\]

  • \(y_i\) is the observation on day \(t_i\)
  • \(f(t_i, \theta)\) is what the model predicts for that day with parameters \(\theta\)

The parameters that make this sum smallest are the least squares estimates

Properties of the squared error criterion

  • Squaring makes every residual positive, so residuals above and below the curve do not cancel
  • Large residuals are penalised much more heavily than small ones
  • The SSE is a differentiable function of \(\theta\), which gradient-based optimisers rely on
  • Under normally distributed errors with constant variance (homoscedasticity), the least squares estimate is also the maximum likelihood estimate

Limitations of the squared error criterion

  • The criterion is sensitive to outliers: a single extreme observation can pull the whole fit towards itself
  • It assumes constant variance, treating a day with 3 pupils in bed the same as a day with 300, which count data rarely satisfy
  • It has no probabilistic interpretation, so it provides no measure of uncertainty on its own

Least squares for the flu outbreak

Step 1: model implementation

library(deSolve)

sir_model <- function(t, y, params) {
  with(as.list(c(y, params)), {
    dS <- -beta * S * I
    dI <- beta * S * I - gamma * I
    dR <- gamma * I
    list(c(dS, dI, dR))
  })
}

N <- 763                            # pupils in the school
init <- c(S = 762, I = 1, R = 0)    # one index case on day 0
times <- 0:14                       # the data are days 1 to 14

# Model prediction of the number of pupils in bed on days 1 to 14
predict_cases <- function(beta, gamma) {
  out <- ode(y = init, times = times, func = sir_model,
             parms = c(beta = beta, gamma = gamma))
  out[-1, "I"]
}

The least squares fit

Strengths of least squares

  • Computationally cheap, with well-established optimisation algorithms available in every statistical package
  • An intuitive criterion that is easy to explain and to implement
  • Under its assumptions the estimates are unbiased, with the minimum variance among linear unbiased estimators
  • Good initial values for the likelihood-based methods that follow, which is how we use it in the next section

Limitations of least squares

  • No uncertainty quantification: the objective function surface was visibly tilted and elongated, but the point estimate carries none of that information
  • No principled basis for model comparison
  • The constant variance assumption is violated by count data, whose variance grows with the mean
  • Parameter identifiability problems are not diagnosed, only hidden in the shape of the surface

Maximum likelihood estimation

The likelihood function

Instead of measuring the distance between the curve and the data, we specify a probability model for the observations and ask how probable the observed counts are under the model with parameters \(\theta\). This probability, viewed as a function of \(\theta\), is the likelihood

\[L(\theta) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} f(y_i \mid \theta)\]

where \(f(y_i \mid \theta)\) is the probability (or density) of observation \(y_i\), and the product follows from assuming the observations are conditionally independent given the model. The maximum likelihood estimate (MLE) is the \(\theta\) that maximises \(L(\theta)\)

The log-likelihood

A product of fourteen small probabilities underflows numerically. Taking logarithms turns the product into a sum, the log-likelihood \(\ell(\theta)\), which has the same maximiser

\[\hat{\theta} = \arg\max_{\theta} \ell(\theta) = \arg\max_{\theta} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \log f(y_i \mid \theta)\]

Optimisation software minimises by convention, so in practice we minimise the negative log-likelihood

Any Questions?

Advantages of maximum likelihood

  • A principled statistical framework: the likelihood follows from an explicit probability model for the data, so every assumption is stated
  • Uncertainty quantification: the curvature of the log-likelihood at its maximum gives standard errors, and the profile likelihood gives confidence intervals
  • Model comparison: models fitted to the same data can be compared through their maximised likelihoods, penalised for complexity by AIC or BIC
  • Flexible observation models: Poisson, negative binomial, normal, or whatever matches how the data arise
  • Asymptotic optimality: with enough data the MLE is consistent, asymptotically normal and efficient

Choosing an observation model

The data are counts, so the natural choices are

  • Poisson: \(Y_i \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda_i)\), a single parameter, with variance equal to the mean (equidispersion)
  • Negative binomial: \(Y_i \sim \text{NB}(\mu_i, \phi)\), with a dispersion parameter \(\phi\) that allows the variance to exceed the mean (overdispersion)

For continuous measurements the usual choices are the normal, \(Y_i \sim N(\mu_i, \sigma^2)\), or the log-normal, \(\log Y_i \sim N(\log \mu_i, \sigma^2)\)

We start with Poisson, and come back to the negative binomial at the end

Discussion

  • Which observation model would you choose for daily deaths? For weekly reported cases with under-reporting? For a serological survey?
  • What goes wrong if you use a Poisson model on data that are overdispersed?

MLE for the flu outbreak: Poisson likelihood

# Negative log-likelihood, with the parameters on the log scale: the optimiser
# can then move freely while beta and gamma stay positive, and the two
# parameters are on a comparable scale
nll_function <- function(log_beta, log_gamma) {
  predicted <- predict_cases(beta = exp(log_beta), gamma = exp(log_gamma))
  -sum(dpois(flu$cases, lambda = predicted, log = TRUE))
}

Strengths of maximum likelihood

  • A principled probabilistic framework, with standard errors and confidence intervals as part of the estimate
  • Formal model comparison through likelihood ratios, AIC and BIC, as we have just used it
  • Observation models that match the data: Poisson counts, overdispersed counts, or continuous measurements
  • Asymptotic optimality: with enough data the estimates are as precise as any unbiased estimator can be

Assumptions and limitations of maximum likelihood

  • An explicit observation model must be specified, and can be misspecified
  • Numerical optimisation requires sensible starting values and can converge to a local maximum; we started from the least squares fit for exactly that reason
  • Standard errors and Wald intervals rest on asymptotic (large-sample) arguments that fourteen observations may not satisfy; the profile likelihood is more reliable
  • Parameter identifiability is not resolved by the method: the profiles were narrow here only because these data are unusually clean

Comparison of methods

When to use each method

Least squares

  • Exploratory analysis, where a quick point estimate is enough
  • Obtaining initial values for likelihood-based or Bayesian methods
  • Situations where computational speed matters and a simple error structure is acceptable

Maximum likelihood

  • Whenever uncertainty quantification is required
  • Model comparison and selection between candidate models
  • Data with overdispersion or other non-constant error structures
  • Formal statistical inference for reports and publications

Bayesian inference and MCMC

From point estimates to posterior distributions

Point estimation: least squares and maximum likelihood

  • One best value for each parameter, \(\hat{\theta}\)
  • Uncertainty came afterwards, from standard errors or the profile likelihood

The Bayesian formulation

So far we have asked which \(\theta\) fits best. A Bayesian asks

**“How plausible is each value of** \(\theta\), given the data?”

  • The answer is a probability distribution over \(\theta\)
  • Every estimate, interval and prediction we report comes from that one distribution

Bayesian inference is reallocation of credibility

  • Before we see any data, each possible value of \(\theta\) has some prior credibility
  • The data then make some values more believable and others less
  • Bayes’ rule is the rule for moving credibility towards the values that explain the data well

Bayesian analysis is reallocation of credibility across possibilities. The rest is details.

Bayes’ rule

\[p(\theta \mid y) = \frac{p(y \mid \theta)\, p(\theta)}{p(y)}\]

  • \(p(\theta)\) is the prior, what we believed before seeing the data \(y\)
  • \(p(y \mid \theta)\) is the likelihood, the same function we maximised in the MLE section
  • \(p(\theta \mid y)\) is the posterior, what we believe now
  • \(p(y)\) is a normalising constant. It is the same number for every \(\theta\), so it does not change the shape of the posterior

\[\text{posterior} \propto \text{likelihood} \times \text{prior}\]

The prior

Sources of prior information

  • Earlier outbreaks and the literature, for example the infectious period of influenza
  • Constraints: rates are positive, proportions lie between 0 and 1
  • Expert judgement: what the people who know the disease think

Vague and informative priors

  • A vague prior spreads credibility widely and leaves the work to the data
  • An informative prior writes down what we already know. It matters most when the data are thin
  • Either way the prior is part of the model, so say what it is, and check what it implies

Prior predictive checks

  • Nobody has intuition for a prior on \(\beta\). Everybody has intuition for an epidemic curve
  • So draw parameter values from the prior, run the model, and look at the epidemics it produces
  • If half of them put the whole school in bed by day two, the prior is telling you something you did not intend

Discussion

  • What prior would you put on the infectious period of influenza? Of Ebola? Where would you get it from?
  • Is a prior that puts the whole school in bed by day two “wrong”, or just vague?

The posterior

Posterior summaries

  • A point estimate, if you want one: the posterior mean or median
  • A 95% credible interval, the range that holds 95% of the posterior probability
  • You can read it literally: *“there is a 95% probability that* \(R_0\) lies between 3.4 and 3.7”. A confidence interval does not allow that sentence
  • Anything you can compute from the parameters, such as \(R_0 = \beta N / \gamma\), comes with its own distribution at no extra cost
  • Predictions that carry the parameter uncertainty through

The posterior is intractable

The normalising constant

\[p(y) = \int p(y \mid \theta)\, p(\theta)\, d\theta\]

  • This is an integral over every combination of parameter values, and for an ODE model there is no formula for it
  • What we can do is evaluate \(p(y \mid \theta)\, p(\theta)\) at any \(\theta\) we like, which gives us the posterior up to that unknown constant

Monte Carlo estimation

  • If you want the mean height of the people of Kumasi you do not measure everyone. You measure a sample
  • A posterior can be treated the same way. Given enough draws of \(\theta\), the mean, the quantiles and the histogram of the sample are as good as the real thing

Sampling from an unnormalised density

  • Monte Carlo needs samples from the posterior
  • All we can do is evaluate the posterior, up to a constant, at any point we choose

How do you draw samples from something you can only evaluate?

Markov chain Monte Carlo

Core idea: generate the samples one after another as a chain, where each new value depends only on the one before it (that is what Markov means), and set up the rule for the next step so that the chain spends time in each region in proportion to the posterior density

  • Start somewhere. Propose a small move. Accept or reject it depending on how the posterior changes. Repeat
  • Run it long enough and the values in the chain are draws from the posterior, and we never had to compute \(p(y)\)
  • The original version is the Metropolis algorithm, which is what we build next

The Metropolis algorithm

  1. Start at some \(\theta^{(0)}\)
  2. Propose \(\theta^* = \theta + \varepsilon\), where \(\varepsilon\) comes from a symmetric proposal distribution such as \(N(0, \sigma^2)\)
  3. Compute \(r = \dfrac{p(\theta^* \mid y)}{p(\theta \mid y)}\). The constant \(p(y)\) appears top and bottom and cancels
  4. Draw \(u \sim U(0, 1)\). If \(u < r\) accept the move (\(\theta \leftarrow \theta^*\)); otherwise stay where you are
  5. Record \(\theta\) and go back to step 2

The acceptance rule

Consequences of the acceptance rule

  • If the proposal has higher posterior density than where we are, we always take it
  • If it has lower density we take it with probability \(r\). The chain can walk downhill, which is why it explores the distribution instead of climbing to the top and staying there
  • When a proposal is rejected the current value is written down again. Those repeats are part of the sample, so do not throw them away
  • Put together, the chain visits each region in proportion to its posterior density

Detailed balance and the stationary distribution

  • The acceptance rule gives the chain detailed balance: as much probability flows from \(\theta\) to \(\theta^*\) as flows back, so nothing piles up anywhere \[p(\theta \mid y)\, q(\theta^* \mid \theta)\, \alpha(\theta, \theta^*) = p(\theta^* \mid y)\, q(\theta \mid \theta^*)\, \alpha(\theta^*, \theta)\]
  • \(\alpha = \min(1, r)\) is the smallest correction that makes this hold
  • The posterior is then the chain’s stationary distribution. Run it long enough and its values are posterior draws
  • With an asymmetric proposal the ratio needs a correction. That is Metropolis–Hastings: \[r = \frac{p(\theta^* \mid y)\, q(\theta \mid \theta^*)}{p(\theta \mid y)\, q(\theta^* \mid \theta)}\]

Implementation in R

metropolis_1d <- function(log_target, n_iter, init, proposal_sd) {
  samples <- numeric(n_iter)
  theta <- init
  log_p <- log_target(theta)
  n_accepted <- 0
  for (i in seq_len(n_iter)) {
    # 1. propose a move: current value plus symmetric noise
    proposal <- theta + rnorm(1, mean = 0, sd = proposal_sd)
    log_p_proposal <- log_target(proposal)
    # 2. accept with probability min(1, p(proposal) / p(theta)); on the log
    #    scale that is: accept if log(u) < log_p_proposal - log_p
    if (log(runif(1)) < log_p_proposal - log_p) {
      theta <- proposal
      log_p <- log_p_proposal
      n_accepted <- n_accepted + 1
    }
    # 3. record where the chain is (the old value again, if we rejected)
    samples[i] <- theta
  }
  list(samples = samples, acceptance_rate = n_accepted / n_iter)
}

Everything is done on the log scale, so the products become sums and the tiny likelihoods do not underflow to zero

Validation on a known target distribution

The sampler knows nothing about Bayes. It will sample from anything you can evaluate, so try it first on \(N(5, 2)\), where we know what the answer should look like.

log_target <- function(theta) dnorm(theta, mean = 5, sd = 2, log = TRUE)

Practical implementation

Tuning the proposal

Proposal scale and acceptance rate

  • Make the proposal too narrow and nearly every move is accepted, but each move is tiny and the chain crawls
  • Make it too wide and nearly every move is rejected, so the chain sits still for long stretches
  • For a random walk proposal, aim for an acceptance rate of about 44% with one parameter, down to about 23% with many
  • If the parameters are correlated in the posterior, the proposal should be correlated the same way

Burn-in

  • We chose the starting point, so the first part of the chain says more about that choice than about the posterior
  • Throw those early samples away. This is the burn-in. Discarding a few extra costs little, and starting from a sensible place such as the MLE shortens it
  • How much to discard is decided after looking at the chain, never before

Convergence assessment with multiple chains

Convergence diagnostics

  • A single chain cannot tell you about the places it has never been
  • So start several chains far apart. If they end up indistinguishable, they have found the same distribution
  • \(\hat{R}\) turns that into a number by comparing the variance between chains with the variance within them. We want \(\hat{R}\) below 1.01
  • Consecutive draws are correlated, so 20,000 of them carry less information than 20,000 independent draws would. The effective sample size is the number that matters
# Gelman-Rubin R-hat: variance between chains relative to variance within them.
# Chains that have found the same distribution give R-hat close to 1.
rhat <- function(chains) {
  n <- length(chains[[1]])
  W <- mean(sapply(chains, var))            # mean within-chain variance
  B <- n * var(sapply(chains, mean))        # n x variance of the chain means
  sqrt(((n - 1) / n * W + B / n) / W)
}

Convergence checklist

  • The trace plots show no trend, the same spread at the start as at the end, and the chains lie on top of each other
  • The acceptance rate is in a sensible range
  • \(\hat{R}\) is close to 1 and the effective sample size is in the hundreds at least
  • Any of these checks can show you a problem. None of them can prove there is not one

Prior, likelihood, posterior

# Sample theta = (log beta, log gamma): the chain roams freely, the rates stay positive

# Prior: normal on the logs (lognormal on beta and gamma), centred on R0 = 2 and a
# 2-day infectious period, with sd 1 on the log scale (a factor of ~2.7 either way)
log_prior <- function(theta) {
  dnorm(theta[1], mean = log(0.0013), sd = 1, log = TRUE) +
    dnorm(theta[2], mean = log(0.5), sd = 1, log = TRUE)
}

# Likelihood: Poisson counts around the model trajectory, as in the MLE section
log_likelihood <- function(theta) {
  predicted <- predict_cases(beta = exp(theta[1]), gamma = exp(theta[2]))
  if (any(!is.finite(predicted)) || any(predicted < 0)) return(-Inf)  # solver hiccup
  sum(dpois(flu$cases, lambda = predicted, log = TRUE))
}

# Posterior up to a constant: all the Metropolis ratio needs
log_posterior <- function(theta) log_prior(theta) + log_likelihood(theta)

The multivariate Metropolis sampler

metropolis <- function(log_target, n_iter, init, proposal_cov) {
  samples <- matrix(NA, nrow = n_iter, ncol = length(init))
  theta <- init
  log_p <- log_target(theta)
  if (!is.finite(log_p)) stop("the starting value has zero posterior density")
  n_accepted <- 0
  for (i in seq_len(n_iter)) {
    proposal <- theta + MASS::mvrnorm(1, mu = rep(0, length(init)),
                                      Sigma = proposal_cov)
    log_p_proposal <- log_target(proposal)
    if (log(runif(1)) < log_p_proposal - log_p) {
      theta <- proposal
      log_p <- log_p_proposal
      n_accepted <- n_accepted + 1
    }
    samples[i, ] <- theta
  }
  list(samples = samples, acceptance_rate = n_accepted / n_iter)
}

Adaptive tuning and multiple chains

# Pilot run: a rough, uncorrelated proposal to find the region of high posterior
pilot <- metropolis(log_posterior, n_iter = 3000,
                    init = c(log(0.0013), log(0.5)),
                    proposal_cov = diag(c(0.1, 0.1)^2))

# Tune: scale the covariance of the pilot samples (Roberts & Rosenthal's 2.38^2/d)
tuned_cov <- 2.38^2 / 2 * cov(pilot$samples[-(1:1000), ])
# Four chains from starting points spread well beyond the posterior (a factor
# of ~1.6 either side of the pilot centre), so that agreement between them means
# something
centre <- colMeans(pilot$samples[-(1:1000), ])
starts <- list(centre + c(-0.5, -0.5), centre + c(0.5, -0.5),
               centre + c(-0.5, 0.5), centre + c(0.5, 0.5))
chains <- lapply(starts, function(s) metropolis(log_posterior, n_iter = 6000,
                                                 init = s, proposal_cov = tuned_cov))
burn_in <- 1000
posterior_samples <- do.call(rbind, lapply(chains, function(ch) ch$samples[-(1:burn_in), ]))

Beyond the Metropolis algorithm

  • Metropolis-within-Gibbs updates one parameter, or one block of parameters, at a time, which helps when the parameters are correlated
  • Adaptive MCMC learns the proposal covariance from the chain while it runs, so you do not have to tune it by hand
  • Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, and its self-tuning version NUTS, uses the gradient of the log posterior to propose long moves in a sensible direction. It is the default sampler in Stan
  • Particle MCMC is for stochastic models, where the likelihood itself has to be estimated by simulation. That is the next section

Probabilistic programming languages

  • You write down the model, the prior and the likelihood. The software does the sampling and reports the diagnostics
  • Stan (through rstan or cmdstanr), nimble, brms, PyMC and Turing.jl all work this way

Example: the SIR model in Stan

// SIR model in Stan (walkthrough)
// Functions block: derivative of [S, I, R]
functions {
  vector SIR(real t, vector y, array[] real theta) {
    real S = y[1]; real I = y[2]; real R = y[3];
    real beta = theta[1]; real gamma = theta[2];
    vector[3] dydt;
    dydt[1] = -beta * S * I;
    dydt[2] =  beta * S * I - gamma * I;
    dydt[3] =  gamma * I;
    return dydt;
  }
}

// Data block: obs counts & time grid
data {
  int<lower=1> n_obs;
  int<lower=1> n_pop;
  array[n_obs] int y;
  real t0;
  array[n_obs] real ts;
}

// Parameters: beta, gamma, S0
parameters {
  array[2] real<lower=0> theta;     // {beta, gamma}
  real<lower=0,upper=1> S0;         // initial susceptible fraction
}

// Transformed params: ODE solve + Poisson rate
transformed parameters {
  vector[3] y_init;
  array[n_obs] vector[3] y_hat;
  array[n_obs] real lambda;
  y_init[1] = S0; y_init[2] = 1 - S0; y_init[3] = 0;
  y_hat = ode_rk45(SIR, y_init, t0, ts, theta);
  for (i in 1:n_obs) lambda[i] = y_hat[i, 2] * n_pop;
}

// Model: priors + likelihood
model {
  theta ~ lognormal(0, 1);
  S0    ~ beta(1, 1);
  y     ~ poisson(lambda);
}

// GQ: derived R0
generated quantities {
  real R_0 = theta[1] / theta[2];
}

The Bayesian workflow

  1. Write down the model and the prior, and check the prior by simulating from it
  2. Write down the likelihood, that is, how the data arise from the model
  3. Sample the posterior with MCMC, using several chains started far apart
  4. Check the chains: trace plots, acceptance rate, \(\hat{R}\), effective sample size
  5. Summarise the posterior: means, credible intervals, and derived quantities such as \(R_0\)
  6. Check the fit by plotting posterior predictions against the data

Advanced methods

Limitations of likelihood-based inference

Everything today assumed we could write down the likelihood and evaluate it quickly. That stops being true when

  • the model is stochastic, so the same parameters give a different epidemic every run
  • the state of the system (how many are infected right now) is itself unknown and has to be estimated along with the parameters
  • the model is expensive enough that even MCMC’s thousands of evaluations are too many
  • estimates are needed as the outbreak unfolds, updated every time new data arrive

Overview of advanced methods

  1. Bayesian inference with MCMC, which we have just done
  2. Particle filtering, for stochastic models where the state is hidden
  3. Approximate Bayesian computation, for when the likelihood cannot be written down at all

And, cutting across all of them, ensembles: combining several models or several fits rather than betting on one

Particle filtering

A particle filter, or sequential Monte Carlo, treats the model as a state-space model: a hidden state (the true numbers of susceptible, infectious and recovered) observed with error. It runs the stochastic model forward as a set of simulations, the particles. At each observation the particles are weighted by their likelihood and resampled, so those consistent with the data are kept and multiplied and the rest are discarded. The surviving particles approximate the filtering distribution of the hidden state given the data so far

When particle filtering is appropriate

It is the tool of choice when

  • the model is stochastic and the noise in transmission matters
  • you need the current state, not just the parameters
  • parameters change over time, or data go missing for a while
  • the answer has to be updated in real time

The filter also yields an unbiased estimate of the marginal likelihood. Using that estimate inside a Metropolis–Hastings sampler gives particle MCMC (pMCMC), which performs full Bayesian inference for the parameters of a stochastic model

Approximate Bayesian computation

When the likelihood is intractable but the model can be simulated from, approximate Bayesian computation (ABC) replaces likelihood evaluation with simulation:

  1. Draw parameters from the prior
  2. Simulate an outbreak with them
  3. Compare the simulated outbreak with the observed one through a distance between summary statistics
  4. Accept the parameters if the distance is below a tolerance \(\epsilon\), reject them otherwise

The accepted parameters are draws from an approximate posterior. The quality of the approximation depends on the summary statistics and the tolerance, and the acceptance rate is typically low, but the method applies to models for which no likelihood can be written

Ensemble methods

Ensemble methods combine several models, methods or fits rather than relying on a single one:

  • model ensembles: average the predictions of several models, weighted or unweighted
  • method ensembles: combine estimates from least squares, MLE and MCMC
  • bootstrap ensembles: refit the same model to resampled versions of the data

The spread across the ensemble quantifies model and method uncertainty, which no single fit can show, and averaged predictions are more robust and less prone to overfitting than any single member

Practical considerations

Common fitting problems

Convergence failure

Usually one of four things:

  • Poor starting values: the search moves into parameter values where the model or the solver breaks down
  • A flat likelihood surface in some direction, so there is no gradient to follow
  • Poorly scaled parameters, as \(\beta\) and \(\gamma\) were today; a log transformation fixed it
  • Numerical instability at parameter bounds, or missing bounds where they were needed

Parameter identifiability problems

  • Multiple solutions: several parameter combinations fit equally well, so the estimate depends on the starting value
  • Correlated parameters: only a combination such as the ratio \(\beta / \gamma\) is determined by the data
  • Insufficient data or an overparameterised model

Remedies for identifiability problems

  • Multiple starting values, checking that they converge to the same estimate
  • Profile likelihoods or posterior distributions for each parameter, not point estimates alone
  • Fixing parameters known from other sources, or placing informative priors on them
  • Additional data that inform the parameters the outbreak curve cannot
  • Model reduction: simplify until the model is identifiable, then add complexity back one piece at a time

Implausible estimates

  • Check the model assumptions against what is known about the disease and the setting
  • Check data quality: reporting changes, backfilled counts, case definitions that changed during the outbreak
  • Residual analysis: a pattern in the residuals over time indicates a missing process in the model
  • Sensitivity analysis: how much the estimates move when uncertain assumptions are changed
  • Fit alternative models and compare them formally

Best practices

Before fitting

  • Plot the data and understand how they were collected
  • Check the model assumptions against the setting
  • Set realistic parameter bounds or priors before fitting
  • Prepare several starting values

While fitting

  • Monitor convergence of the optimiser or the chain, rather than reading only the final estimate
  • Check for local optima by comparing runs from different starting values
  • Document every fit, including the ones that failed

After fitting

  • Assess goodness of fit by plotting the fitted model over the data with its uncertainty
  • Report confidence or credible intervals, not point estimates alone
  • Carry out a sensitivity analysis of the conclusions to the assumptions
  • Validate predictions on data not used in fitting

Conclusions

Key takeaways

Least squares

Fast and intuitive. It provides a point estimate and initial values for other methods, but no uncertainty quantification and no basis for model comparison

Maximum likelihood

A principled statistical framework built on an explicit observation model. It provides confidence intervals and formal model comparison, at the cost of specifying that observation model and managing a numerical optimiser

Bayesian inference and MCMC

  • Priors make assumptions explicit and allow them to be checked by prior predictive simulation
  • The posterior distribution is the complete answer: point estimates, credible intervals and predictions all come from the same samples
  • The Metropolis algorithm is a few lines of code; the effort goes into tuning the proposal and diagnosing convergence
  • Never trust a chain whose diagnostics you have not examined

Advanced methods

Particle filtering, approximate Bayesian computation and ensemble methods handle stochastic models, intractable likelihoods and model uncertainty. They require more expertise and more computation than anything covered today

Choosing a method

  • Exploratory analysis: least squares
  • Formal inference for a report or publication: maximum likelihood
  • Many parameters, or prior information to incorporate: Bayesian inference with MCMC
  • Stochastic models, hidden states, or real-time estimation: particle filtering
  • Intractable likelihoods: approximate Bayesian computation

Whatever the problem, start simple and add complexity only when the simple version fails you

Closing remarks

Model fitting requires domain expertise, statistical rigour and careful validation, all at once

The fit itself is not the goal. The goal is to understand the transmission dynamics, to make reliable predictions, and to inform the decisions that depend on them
